## Executive Summary

Civic Forensics is an independent civic intelligence initiative developed in Bosnia and Herzegovina. It creates practical methods, open publications and AI-assisted tools that help citizens, journalists, civil society organisations and public-interest professionals examine official decisions, institutional responses, public statements, media reports and quantitative claims.

The initiative addresses a recurring democratic problem: information may be formally public while remaining difficult to understand, verify or use. Official communication can contain unsupported conclusions, incomplete evidence, procedural formalism, ambiguous responsibility or numerical claims presented without sufficient context. At the same time, artificial intelligence is entering public communication and public administration faster than most citizens can develop the literacy needed to assess its use.

Civic Forensics responds by converting complex public material into structured and reproducible oversight processes. Its operational cycle is:

> **Evidence → Verification → Analysis → Publication → Civic Action → Institutional Response → Learning**

The initiative does not prescribe political conclusions. It separates facts, legal rules, institutional claims, interpretation, inference and allegation. AI output is treated as analytical assistance rather than evidence or an authoritative legal conclusion.

The current ecosystem includes bilingual publication platforms, an open methodology repository, case-based analyses, an AI prompt library, the Democratic Resilience & AI Literacy Program, the EU Compliance Watch publication system, the first Article 50 Deep Dive and research concepts and prototypes for a Civic Intelligence Dashboard.

Civic Forensics operates complementary Serbian- and English-language publication platforms. The Serbian-language platform focuses on civic oversight, public accountability and citizen tools in Bosnia and Herzegovina, while the English-language platform presents methodologies, publications and European regulatory analyses for an international audience. Together, these platforms connect local civic practice with broader European policy discussions.

Civic Forensics is seeking dialogue and cooperation with organisations working on democratic resilience, civil society, public-administration reform, AI literacy, digital rights, media and information literacy, open government, transparency and responsible digital transformation.

The immediate development opportunity is a structured pilot that combines civic education, regulatory monitoring, open-source tools, case-based learning and publication infrastructure. The purpose of a first partnership is not to replace existing institutions or watchdog organisations, but to provide a reusable civic-intelligence layer that helps diverse actors convert public information into verifiable evidence and lawful follow-up.

## 1. Why Civic Forensics

Public information is often available but not practically usable. Citizens encounter administrative decisions, procurement notices, public-company reports, urban-planning documents, institutional statements and numerical claims that require legal, procedural, financial or technical knowledge to interpret.

Administrative formalism can simulate accountability. An institution may respond within a formal process while avoiding the central question, citing competence or procedure without connecting the reply to the requested evidence.

Quantitative claims can create false certainty. Percentages, rankings, totals and performance indicators may conceal the denominator, comparison group, methodology, exclusions or the distinction between activity and outcome.

Artificial intelligence creates a new civic-literacy gap. AI can improve access to information, but it can also obscure authorship, responsibility, source quality and the boundary between human and automated judgement.

European standards therefore need translation into civic practice: what changed, who is responsible, which evidence should exist, which questions can be asked and how formal compliance can be distinguished from meaningful implementation.

## 2. Mission, Vision and Values

### Mission

To strengthen citizens’ ability to examine public decisions and institutional claims through transparent methods, verifiable evidence, responsible use of AI and practical civic-oversight tools.

### Vision

Public institutions should be transparent not only because information is published, but because important claims, decisions and procedures can be independently understood, tested and traced to evidence.

### Institutional descriptor

**Civic Forensics — Independent Civic Intelligence Initiative**

Civic Forensics is not presented as a public authority, regulator, accredited research institute or registered international organisation. It is an independent public-interest initiative building an open methodology, publication infrastructure and practical civic-learning ecosystem.

### Core values

1. **Evidence before assurance.** Institutional confidence does not replace documentation.
2. **Verification before conclusion.** A plausible claim is not automatically a proven claim.
3. **Neutral method, public-interest purpose.** The same analytical standard applies regardless of institution, party, official or media source.
4. **Clear limits.** Unknown facts, alternative explanations and evidentiary gaps are stated explicitly.
5. **Responsible AI.** AI supports extraction, comparison and question generation; it does not become the source of truth.
6. **Reproducibility.** Readers should be able to understand how a conclusion was reached.
7. **Lawful civic action.** Analysis should connect to proportionate and lawful follow-up options.

## 4. Civic Forensics Methodology

The methodology organises analysis around six questions:

1. What exactly is being claimed or decided?
2. Which facts and evidence support the claim?
3. Which institution or official has legal and practical responsibility?
4. Which procedure, standard or rule applies?
5. What remains unverified or open to another explanation?
6. What proportionate follow-up can produce additional evidence or accountability?

A publication is not treated as the end of the process. A new official response, court decision, inspection record or disclosed document becomes a new evidence input. Previous conclusions can then be marked as confirmed, amended, disproved or supplemented.

- **ECW-CG** — Citizen Guide;

### First Deep Dive

**ECW-DD-001 — Article 50 Is Now Enforceable: A Civic Guide to AI Transparency**

**Status:** Published output, with the final stable portal URL and repository release record to be confirmed in the final CFP-001 edition.

The publication explains Article 50 of the EU AI Act from the perspective of citizens, journalists, auditors and public authorities. It demonstrates the Civic Forensics approach: moving from legal obligation to verifiable evidence, compliance indicators, red flags and practical questions.

### Case studies and explanatory publications

Civic Forensics develops case-based analyses concerning official decisions, institutional responses, local government, public services, public communication, quantitative claims and access to information.

Publication requires privacy protection, source preservation, legal-risk review and clear separation between proven facts, interpretation and allegation.

## 9. Pilot Opportunities

### Proposed working title

**Democratic Resilience & AI Literacy Pilot: Civic Oversight in the Age of AI**

### Proposed duration

12 months, subject to partner consultation and available support.

### Geographic approach

Initial implementation in Bosnia and Herzegovina, with materials designed for regional and European reuse.

### Proposed activities

1. Validate and adapt the Civic Forensics methodology with external experts.
2. Produce English and local-language AI literacy and civic-oversight materials.
3. Develop structured case-based learning modules.
4. Publish a regular EU Compliance Watch cycle.
5. Train a pilot group of users and facilitators.
6. Test evidence registers, checklists and open-source analytical prompts.
7. Develop and test a limited Civic Intelligence Dashboard prototype.
8. Document learning, corrections, safeguards and user feedback.
9. Produce an evaluation and sustainability plan.

### Proposed outputs

- 4 Deep Dive publications;
- 12 Watch Reports;
- 8 Citizen Guides;
- 12 Citizen Checklists;
- structured learning modules and exercises;
- facilitator materials;
- open-source prompt and evidence templates;
- pilot dashboard prototype;
- evaluation report and lessons-learned publication.

These figures are proposed pilot targets, not current achievements.

### Safeguards

- political and institutional neutrality of the analytical method;
- explicit separation of fact, interpretation, inference and allegation;
- privacy protection and data minimisation;
- human editorial responsibility;
- source and evidence-gap disclosure;
- right of reply and correction procedures;
- legal-risk and safeguarding review;
- no automated legal determination without evidence;
- transparent disclosure of AI assistance.
